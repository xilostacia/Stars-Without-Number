The **Chorus** are an exceptionally rare sophont species encountered sporadically throughout inhabited space. A Chorus appears at first glance to be a single organism, but examination has shown that its body is actually composed of an enormous number of much smaller living organisms functioning together as a coordinated whole.

Whether the individual organisms making up a Chorus possess any degree of independent intelligence is unknown. What is clear is that the colony as a whole behaves with deliberate intent, demonstrates awareness of its surroundings, reacts appropriately to danger, and is capable of navigating technological environments that would be meaningless to a nonsapient animal. No known species has established reliable communication with them.

Despite this, the Chorus do not appear hostile. Most encounters consist of a Chorus arriving somewhere, wandering through the area for some period of time, sometimes boarding a ship or station transit system, and eventually leaving without explanation. Nobody knows why.

## Biology
A Chorus is not a single animal in the conventional sense. Its visible body is a colony made from an enormous number of smaller organisms acting together. These organisms cooperate closely enough that the resulting structure moves, reacts, observes, and behaves as though it were one individual.

The exact number of organisms comprising a Chorus has never been reliably determined. Estimates vary dramatically depending upon the individual being examined and upon what researchers consider a separate organism rather than a specialized component of the greater colony.

The individual organisms are small enough that the boundary between "body" and "population" becomes difficult to define. Some appear specialized for movement, others for sensory processing, structural support, protection, chemical exchange, or other functions. Whether these are permanent biological castes or temporary roles assumed as needed remains uncertain.

A Chorus does not possess a fixed anatomy in the same sense as a human, Raukha, or most other familiar sophonts. Its external shape is relatively stable over short periods, enough that an observer can recognize the same Chorus from one moment to the next, but internal structures can apparently be redistributed. Parts of the colony can move, compress, expand, separate locally, and reorganize without obviously harming the greater organism.

Researchers have therefore struggled to identify permanent equivalents to organs. A structure functioning as a lung, sensory organ, support structure, or circulatory system during one observation may no longer occupy the same location during another. Exactly how far this reorganization can go is unknown.

## Appearance
There is no single standard Chorus body plan. Most observed individuals maintain a roughly coherent central mass with several appendage-like extensions used for movement and manipulation, but even these features vary. Some appear tall and narrow, others broad and low, while others display body shapes that do not correspond well to familiar terrestrial animals.

The surface of a Chorus is visibly organic but difficult to classify. Depending upon the individual, it may appear smooth, fibrous, layered, granular, membranous, or covered in countless small structures whose individual movement becomes apparent only at close range.

Coloration also varies. The overall impression is not normally that of a swarm. At ordinary viewing distance, a Chorus generally looks like a single strange organism. Only close observation reveals that portions of the body are constantly shifting relative to one another. There is no confirmed evidence that outward body shape corresponds to age, sex, social role, ancestry, or any other familiar biological category.

## Identity
One of the largest unanswered questions surrounding the Chorus is whether each visible colony should actually be considered an individual. A Chorus behaves consistently enough that spacers usually treat one colony as one person. A Chorus that boards a ship will maintain apparently coherent goals, remember routes through the vessel, react to events that occurred earlier, and continue pursuing whatever unexplained activity brought it aboard.

That does not necessarily mean human assumptions about individuality apply. No one knows whether the organisms within a colony share information continuously, whether consciousness is distributed throughout the body, whether intelligence emerges only once a colony reaches sufficient size, or whether some smaller component acts as a central coordinating organism.

It is also unknown whether two Chorus colonies that meet remain separate minds. There are poorly documented reports of Chorus touching, partially merging, separating, exchanging material, or remaining physically interconnected for extended periods. No observer has been able to determine whether such events represent reproduction, communication, trade, recognition, memory exchange, or something entirely unrelated.

It is therefore possible that the thing outsiders call "a Chorus" is merely one temporary configuration of something much larger. There is currently no way to know.

## Intelligence
The Chorus are unquestionably sophonts. Their intelligence is most obvious not through communication but through behavior. They understand artificial environments. Chorus have been observed waiting for airlocks to cycle rather than attempting to force their way through sealed doors, moving through queues instead of simply pushing past them, avoiding dangerous machinery, recognizing active transportation systems, boarding the correct side of transit vehicles, navigating stations, and reacting appropriately to emergencies.

They can distinguish between areas they are permitted to enter and areas where their presence causes alarm, although they do not always appear interested in respecting those distinctions. A Chorus placed aboard a ship does not behave like an animal accidentally trapped inside a technological environment. It behaves like something that understands what a ship is. They have been observed examining equipment, watching crews perform maintenance, following individuals through complicated environments, and waiting for specific events before continuing onward.

This behavior leaves little serious scientific doubt that they are intelligent. The difficulty is determining what they are thinking.

## Communication
No known civilization has successfully established meaningful communication with the Chorus. This is not for lack of effort.

Researchers have attempted spoken languages, written languages, mathematical sequences, pictograms, symbolic logic, light patterns, radio signals, chemical communication, tactile patterns, machine translation, behavioral association, and numerous other methods.

The Chorus generally respond by doing nothing. Whether this means they cannot understand these attempts is uncertain. Their behavior occasionally suggests that they understand considerably more than they acknowledge. A Chorus may react appropriately to something said nearby, move after being informed that an area is closing, or stop before entering a dangerous location immediately after being warned. The same Chorus may then completely ignore a direct question, repeated gestures, visual symbols, and attempts at basic yes-or-no communication.

This has produced endless arguments among xenologists. Some believe communication is occurring through a medium other species have not discovered. Others believe the Chorus understand outside communication perfectly well but simply have little interest in responding. Another possibility is that their concept of communication is so different from that of conventional individual organisms that researchers are not even recognizing the relevant behavior.

There is no confirmed answer.

## Vocalization
Chorus are capable of producing sound. The significance of those sounds is unknown. Observed vocalizations include humming, clicking, buzzing, layered tones, low vibrations, rapid pulses, and complex overlapping noises apparently produced by different portions of the colony simultaneously.

This behavior is the origin of the common name **Chorus**.

Whether these sounds constitute language has never been demonstrated. Recordings made during encounters show recurring patterns, and individual Chorus sometimes produce similar sequences under similar circumstances, but attempts to associate those patterns reliably with objects, actions, individuals, or concepts have failed.

Some xenologists argue that the sounds may be incidental products of internal coordination rather than communication directed toward outsiders. Others believe outsiders are hearing only a tiny portion of a much more complicated system.

## Travel
One of the strangest recurring behaviors of the Chorus is their tendency to travel aboard ships belonging to other species. A Chorus may appear in a starport, move through customs or cargo areas, approach a departing vessel, and simply board if nobody stops it. Once aboard, it might remain for several hours, several days, or multiple jumps. It may wander through accessible areas of the ship, remain stationary for long periods, observe crew members, inspect equipment, or simply occupy some apparently arbitrary location. Eventually it leaves.

There is rarely any obvious connection between its point of origin and destination. A Chorus may disembark at a major industrial station, an agricultural colony, a nearly abandoned refueling point, or a world with no obvious reason to interest it. Others have been seen transferring between ships as though following an itinerary nobody else can perceive.

They do not purchase tickets. They do not request passage. They do not offer payment. They also do not generally interfere with shipboard operations. Most experienced crews simply accept that they have acquired a temporary passenger.

## Spacer Etiquette
A collection of informal customs has developed around encounters with the Chorus.
The most important rule is simple: **Leave them alone.**

A Chorus walking through a corridor is generally allowed to continue walking. If it boards a vessel and is not damaging anything or entering a genuinely dangerous area, experienced crews usually permit it to remain aboard. Attempts to restrain, interrogate, capture, dissect, tag, or aggressively redirect a Chorus are considered both dangerous and extremely poor spacer behavior.

This is not because the Chorus are known to retaliate. Quite the opposite.

They almost never display obvious aggression, which means nobody knows what an actually threatened Chorus is capable of doing. More practically, harassing an apparently peaceful sophont for no reason is considered unacceptable throughout most inhabited space.

Crews who regularly operate along routes where Chorus sightings occur sometimes leave doors open where practical, avoid sealing them inside compartments unnecessarily, and allow them to disembark whenever they choose. Nobody expects payment. Nobody asks where they are going. A Chorus is treated less like a stowaway and more like weather that has briefly decided to travel with you.

## Danger
There are no reliably documented cases of an unprovoked Chorus attack. This does not mean they are harmless. Their unusual biology makes conventional assumptions about injury unreliable, and the physical capabilities of a Chorus have never been properly established. A creature capable of completely reorganizing its own body may possess defensive abilities that have simply never been observed.

Individual organisms can apparently survive damage that would disable a conventional animal, while the colony can redistribute around damaged regions. How much injury is required to kill a Chorus is unknown. Whether "kill" is even the appropriate biological term is also uncertain. Station authorities generally treat them as peaceful sophonts rather than hazardous organisms, while still recommending that personnel avoid physical interference.

## Disease and Contamination
Early encounters generated considerable concern that Chorus organisms might represent a biological contamination hazard. So far, there is little evidence supporting that fear. The constituent organisms appear strongly dependent upon the colony environment and have not been observed establishing independent populations aboard ships, stations, or inhabited worlds.

Material occasionally separated from a Chorus generally dies, becomes inert, or is reabsorbed. No confirmed Chorus-related epidemic has ever been documented. Standard quarantine procedures are nevertheless commonly applied during first contact with a previously unknown Chorus population, particularly on worlds with delicate native biospheres.

## Reproduction
Nobody knows how the Chorus reproduce. No confirmed juvenile Chorus has been identified. There are no confirmed eggs, larvae, pregnancies, spawning grounds, reproductive structures, or developmental stages. It is possible that new Chorus form when existing colonies divide. It is equally possible that the constituent organisms reproduce individually and gradually create a new colony.

Some researchers have proposed that the rare observed mergers between Chorus may be connected to reproduction, while others believe entire populations might participate in reproductive events occurring somewhere beyond known space. There is currently no evidence strong enough to establish any theory as correct.

## Homeworld
The Chorus homeworld is unknown. No recognized Chorus government has identified one, and no expedition has discovered a world conclusively demonstrated to be their point of origin. Chorus individuals have been encountered far apart from one another, sometimes in regions with no obvious historical connection. Attempts to trace their movements backward have produced no consistent result. Some appear aboard vessels whose previous ports contain no record of them arriving. Others are first recorded on isolated stations despite no known ship having transported them there. This has produced claims that the Chorus possess their own unknown form of transportation. There is no confirmed evidence for this.

## Civilization
Nobody knows what Chorus civilization looks like. There are no recognized Chorus cities, states, corporations, militaries, ships, embassies, or colonies. This absence is particularly strange because the Chorus clearly understand advanced technology. Whether they manufacture technology of their own is unknown. A civilization composed of colony organisms may also organize itself in ways outsiders would fail to recognize. What appears to another species as an ecosystem, reef, forest, subterranean colony, or biological landscape could conceivably represent a complex Chorus settlement. Even the assumption that Chorus civilization consists of separate individuals cooperating with one another may be incorrect. There may be millions of independent Chorus.

## Scientific Study
The Chorus are one of the most frustrating subjects in modern xenology. There is enough information to establish that they are biological, intelligent, aware of technology, capable of intentional travel, and apparently peaceful. Almost everything beyond that remains uncertain.

Ethical restrictions also severely limit research. Because the Chorus are recognized as sophonts, invasive experimentation is illegal in most jurisdictions regardless of whether researchers can communicate with them.

Scientists are therefore largely dependent upon observation, voluntary proximity, abandoned biological material, and whatever information can be collected when a Chorus happens to remain in one location long enough to study. The Chorus themselves appear indifferent to this attention.

## Theories
There are countless theories concerning the Chorus. Some researchers believe each visible colony is an ordinary individual whose cognition simply emerges from millions of cooperating organisms. Others suggest every Chorus may be a fragment of a larger distributed consciousness extending across multiple colonies. Another theory proposes that the Chorus encountered by other species are not adults at all, but temporary exploratory forms created by a much larger organism or civilization. More extreme theories claim the Chorus are biological probes, living computers, remnants of an ancient engineered species, or sensory extensions of an organism spread across an entire planetary ecosystem.

## Relations with Other Species
It is difficult to describe the Chorus as having diplomatic relations with anyone. Governments generally classify them as independent sophonts and extend the same basic protections given to other intelligent species, but there are no known treaties, diplomatic missions, territorial negotiations, or formal exchanges involving Chorus representatives. Their relationship with ordinary spacers is considerably simpler.

They occasionally appear. They travel. People leave them alone. Eventually they disappear again. For most inhabitants of known space, encountering a Chorus is a story worth telling for the rest of one's life rather than a political event.
