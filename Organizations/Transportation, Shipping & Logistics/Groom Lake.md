**Groom Lake** is one of humanity’s oldest surviving aerospace and infrastructure companies, tracing its institutional history back to the classified military research facilities once operated at Groom Lake on Earth. The modern company is best known for constructing the **first seven human relay gates**, installations that formed some of the earliest permanent links between inhabited star systems.

Those original relays are unmistakably old. Where modern gates tend to be compact and comparatively refined, Groom Lake’s first-generation installations are enormous industrial complexes surrounded by power systems, docking structures, maintenance yards, traffic infrastructure, control facilities, warehouses, and generations of later additions. Much of the machinery was built before relay engineering became a mature discipline, resulting in systems that are bulky, redundant, expensive to operate, and extraordinarily durable.

All **seven original Groom Lake relays** remain in service and under company control. Groom Lake no longer dominates relay construction, as other governments and manufacturers eventually developed their own designs, but the company continues to maintain its original network and the vast infrastructure that has grown around it. Several of these sites now function as major dockyards, repair centers, transfer stations, and industrial facilities in addition to their original purpose.

Two Groom Lake relays are located within the **Sol System**. The primary Mars installation is a large orbital complex surrounded by shipyards, service docks, storage facilities, and heavy maintenance infrastructure. It has become one of Groom Lake’s most important operational centers and serves as the principal connection between Earth and the wider relay network.

The second is the **Earth Relay**, located at Groom Lake itself in the Nevada desert. Unlike the company’s other gates, the Earth installation sits directly on a planetary surface and connects only to the Mars Relay. Transit between the two is effectively immediate, with travelers experiencing little more than a brief moment of disorientation before emerging at the opposite gate.

The Earth Relay is also considerably smaller than the other original installations. Only relatively small spacecraft can pass through it, forcing larger vessels to remain near Mars and transfer passengers, personnel, or cargo to smaller craft before continuing to Earth. As a result, Mars effectively serves as Earth’s interstellar port even though Groom Lake itself remains the final point of arrival.

The modern Groom Lake facility bears little resemblance to the isolated military base from which the company originated. The surrounding desert now contains enormous hangars, spacecraft landing areas, maintenance complexes, logistics yards, hardened technical facilities, warehouses, research buildings, and the support infrastructure required to operate one of humanity’s oldest functioning relays. Older structures still survive inside the complex, leaving layers of ancient Earth aerospace architecture buried beneath centuries of expansion.

Groom Lake’s business has gradually shifted away from constructing new relays. Much of its modern revenue comes from **relay maintenance, heavy spacecraft servicing, dockyard operations, specialized fabrication, traffic support, logistics, and technical engineering**. Its facilities are particularly valued for work involving older spacecraft, unusual systems, or infrastructure that newer commercial yards are poorly equipped to service.

The company also retains a substantial research division. Groom Lake has accumulated technical archives stretching back to the earliest period of human spaceflight, and its engineers have a reputation for maintaining equipment and solving problems that younger organizations consider obsolete, uneconomical, or simply strange.

Despite operating as a corporation, Groom Lake still carries much of the culture of the secretive military programs from which it descended. Access to many facilities is tightly controlled, internal divisions are heavily compartmentalized, and the company rarely discusses research projects until they become operational.

For most travelers, however, Groom Lake is known for something much simpler: **seven enormous old relay complexes that have been operating since humanity first learned how to build them, and a company that has spent generations making sure they continue to work.**
